The Halvorsen attractor is a continuous-time 3D chaotic flow whose equations cycle under \((x,y,z)\to(y,z,x)\). Swap the axes that way and the rules look the same. The cloud on the stage does not: one lobe sits heavier than the rest, and the trail reads as a skewed knot rather than a three-fold snowflake.

Classical Chaos integrates it the same way as Lorenz or Aizawa: Euler steps into a cool-to-warm line trail. Watch it grow in below.

## Where it came from

Arne Dehli Halvorsen floated the system on sci.fractals (unpublished). J. C. Sprott later wrote it up in *Chaos and Time-Series Analysis* (2003) and again in *Elegant Chaos* (2010), which is how most demos found it.

## Halvorsen’s cyclic ODEs

One free parameter `a`. The linear coupling is fixed at 4.

<BlockMath math={'\\dot{x} = -a x - 4 y - 4 z - y^{2}'} />
<BlockMath math={'\\dot{y} = -a y - 4 z - 4 x - z^{2}'} />
<BlockMath math={'\\dot{z} = -a z - 4 x - 4 y - x^{2}'} />

Read the first line, then rotate every letter one slot: \(x\to y\), \(y\to z\), \(z\to x\). You get the second line. Rotate again and you get the third. That cyclic lock is the whole design.

Chaos often shows up near \(a \approx 1.27\); demos commonly stick with \(a = 1.4\) so the trail fills without dying or blowing up.

## Symmetry in the algebra, not the picture

The equations treat the three axes as equals. The path does not. From seed `(-5, 0, 0)` the trail settles into a bounded chaotic set that looks lopsided from most viewing angles: a bright arm, a quieter fold, no obvious \(120^\circ\) repeat in the silhouette.

That gap is worth sitting with. Cyclic symmetry says the rules are indifferent to which axis is which. The state you happen to be in, and how long you integrate, still pick a particular tangle. Change `a` a little and the tangle fattens or thins, but the three-fold rule stays written into every step.
