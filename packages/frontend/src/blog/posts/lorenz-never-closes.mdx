Three coupled equations, one seed, and a trajectory that folds forever without repeating. Lorenz’s 1963 convection model is the textbook strange attractor — deterministic, bounded, and sensitive to the tiniest nudge.

## Chaotic ≠ random

Every point on the butterfly is the result of an ordinary differential equation. There is no noise term. What looks like chance is sensitivity: two seeds that differ by `1e-8` diverge until they occupy opposite wings.

```
dx/dt = σ(y − x)
dy/dt = x(ρ − z) − y
dz/dt = xy − βz
```

With the classic constants `σ = 10`, `ρ = 28`, `β = 8/3`, the flow stays inside a bounded region of phase space yet never settles onto a closed orbit. That combination — bounded + aperiodic — is the signature of a strange attractor.

## Why it never closes

A periodic orbit would return to the exact same `(x, y, z)` after a finite time. Lorenz’s trajectory comes arbitrarily close to past states and then peels away. Numerically we integrate with a fixed step; the picture is an approximation, but the topology survives: two lobes joined at a saddle.

> The “never closes” claim is about the continuous flow, not about floating-point loops. Finite precision will eventually cycle — the attractor itself does not.
