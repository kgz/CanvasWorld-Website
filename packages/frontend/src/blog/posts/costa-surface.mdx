Before Costa (1984), the known complete embedded minimal surfaces in three-space with finite topology were basically the plane, the catenoid, and the helicoid. Celso Costa wrote down a genus-one surface with three ends. Hoffman and Meeks later proved it does not cross itself — the Costa–Hoffman–Meeks surface.

Classical Chaos draws a truncated piece as a lit triangle mesh: two catenoid-like ends and a flatter middle end, cut short so the infinite flutes stay off-screen.

## Three punctures on a square torus

The chart lives on a square torus: the unit square in the complex plane, identified on opposite sides, with three punctures at <InlineMath math={'0'} />, <InlineMath math={'1/2'} />, and <InlineMath math={'i/2'} />. Those punctures are where the surface runs off to infinity.

Coordinates use Weierstrass <InlineMath math={'\\zeta'} /> and <InlineMath math={'\\wp'} /> on the square lattice <InlineMath math={'\\mathbb{Z}[i]'} />. One height formula that already shows the middle end is

<BlockMath math={'x_3 = \\frac{\\sqrt{2\\pi}}{4}\\log\\left|\\frac{\\wp(z)-e_1}{\\wp(z)+e_1}\\right|,\\quad z = u+iv'} />

with <InlineMath math={'e_1 = \\wp(1/2)'} />. The horizontal coordinates mix <InlineMath math={'\\zeta'} /> at <InlineMath math={'z'} />, <InlineMath math={'z-1/2'} />, and <InlineMath math={'z-i/2'} /> the same way Gray and later notes write them.

Mean curvature stays zero. Globally you still get a torus with three ends, not a closed blob.

## Truncation and the full stage

The canvas never draws the infinite ends. A <InlineMath math={'\\mathrm{margin}'} /> pulls the UV square away from the punctures so the mesh stays finite. Smaller margin means longer flutes; larger margin cuts closer to the neck.
